Your instinct is right — with one condition

Yield on cost (YoC) divides today's dividend by the price you originally paid. It rises on its own, and the capital gain arrives alongside it, for one reason only.

  • 1
    YoC climbs only as fast as the dividend per share climbs.

    A rising share price does nothing to your yield on cost — your cost never changes. If DPS is flat, your YoC is flat forever, no matter what the chart does.

  • 2
    The capital gain is the same engine, seen from the other end.

    The market prices an income stream to a yield it finds acceptable. If it keeps demanding 4.00% and the dividend doubles, the price must double. Dividend growth is the capital gain.

  • 3
    100% YoC is arithmetic, not magic — and it is slow.

    Getting from a 4.00% entry yield to 100% means the dividend must grow 25-fold. At 7.00% DPS growth that is 47.6 years. At Bursa's more typical 3.00%, it is 109 years.

  • 4
    Reinvesting changes the maths entirely.

    Ploughing the dividends back buys more shares, so your income grows at roughly DPS growth + current yield. That 47.6-year wait drops to about 26 years. Toggle DRIP below.

YoC(t) = DPS₀ × (1 + g)^t ÷ Price₀  →  years to target = ln(target ÷ entry yield) ÷ ln(1 + g)

How to read this, and what it is not

Data provenance. This machine had no network access to Bursa Malaysia, Yahoo Finance or any price vendor, so it is not built on downloaded price and dividend files. Each stock carries a hand-set long-run dividend profile — entry yield, DPS growth rate, growth volatility, and the annual odds and depth of a cut — calibrated to how that company and its sector have historically paid. Treat the ranking as a structural map of Bursa's dividend landscape, not as an audited backtest, and re-run it against real DPS histories before putting money behind it.

Years to target is the median year in which the annual dividend, measured against your original cost, first equals or exceeds your target. "Never" means more than half the runs never got there inside 40 years.

Chance of getting there is the share of the 300 runs that reached the target within your holding period. A high yielder with a fragile payout can rank fast on the median and still show a poor chance — that gap is the whole point of the column.

Capital multiple is the share price at your horizon divided by your entry price. It is driven by dividend growth: the simulated market yield mean-reverts to each stock's long-run norm, so a dividend that doubles drags the price with it. That is the mechanism behind your question — the same growth pays you twice.

DRIP reinvests each dividend into more shares at that year's price, so income on your original capital compounds at roughly dividend growth plus current yield. It is the only realistic route to a 100% yield on cost inside one investing lifetime on this market.

Simulation seeded per stock, so the same settings always produce the same numbers. Not investment advice.
